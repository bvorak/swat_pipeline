
## High-flow versus baseflow flow-regimes.

The scientific literature consistently shows that nutrient loads and concentrations in rivers reach their highest values during high-flow (storm) events (e.g. Kelly et al. 2019, Zhang et al. 2024). This has been attributed to enhanced hydrological connectivity and the activation of rapid transport pathways during storm events, promoting nitrogen losses via leaching and phosphorus losses via erosion and sediment mobilization. Concentrations generally peak on the rising limb or near peak flow, with phosphorus showing an early, sharp peak due to erosion and sediment mobilization, and nitrogen exhibiting more variable timing depending on subsurface transport contributions (Bowes et al. 2005; Bieroza and Heathwaite 2015). The experimental dataset was subdivided into two subsets: one representing baseflow (low-flow) conditions and the other corresponding to high-flow (storm event) conditions (see Figure 2). This subdivision was applied to account for flow-dependent differences in the relationships between variables. Storm events (high-flow conditions) here were identified by periods of at least two consecutive days during which flow exceeds the 75th percentile of SWAT simulated flows (Q75pctile = X m3/d) and had a two-day buffer added before and after these identified periods. So, three days with flows above the 75th percentile was interpreted as a 7-day flow event. Of the 14245 simulated days ~28% were within such periods. All other days (~72%) were treated as baseflow (low-flow) conditions. In general, available field data are biased and predominantly represent baseflow conditions. For example, almost 2/3 of the available water quality data for TN correspond to baseflow conditions, the remaining representing observations under high-flows (not necessarily peak-flows). Simulated and observed flow rates are plotted in Fig. 12. Coloured bands identify high- and base-flow periods. Note that, in general, SWAT tends to underestimate the observed flow rates (even at monthly scales). Differences between observed and simulated flows are maximal during wet periods (see, for example, year 1997). Peak flows also tend to be underestimated by the model. ## Sensitivity to pollution sources and flow-regimes

The relative influence of point-source and diffuse nutrient inputs on simulated nutrient concentrations upstream of the Cubillas Reservoir was quantified using a structured perturbation analysis. In this framework, point-source discharges and diffuse (nonpoint) inputs were systematically varied between their minimum and maximum values (input bounds). Three minimum-maximum scenarios were considered. First, .dat point-source inputs were varied between their minimum and maximum bounds while diffuse inputs were where not changed from the SWAT defaults. This will be referred to as the WASTE (for wastewater) scenario. In the second one (referred to as SOIL – for soil-initialization - scenario), diffuse inputs were varied while point sources remained. Finally, in the third scenario (W+S), both point- and diffuse-source inputs were simultaneously varied across their respective ranges. For each scenario, simulations conducted with minimum and maximum input conditions resulted in prediction envelopes representing the range of model outputs under input uncertainty. The relative importance of point and diffuse sources, as well as their combined effects, on model response (measured in terms of loads or concentrations) was assessed by comparing the width and divergence of those envelopes for total phosphorus (TP) and total nitrogen (TN) among scenarios. Differences in envelope width (or spread) among scenarios were used to infer source dominance and potential interactions between point and diffuse inputs. In simple terms, the comparison between the three scenarios tells us where most of the influence on P or N concentration in the model is coming from, i.e.
- WASTE (W) scenario (point sources only): If the range of nutrient concentrations (the envelope) were wide in this scenario, it would be an indication that point sources (wastewater discharges) strongly control P/N concentrations
- SOIL (S) scenario (diffuse sources only): If the range is wide in this case, it means that runoff from land (e.g., agriculture, soil erosion) is the main driver of nutrients concentrations in the river and loads into the reservoir.
- W+S scenario (point and diffuse together): Comparing the results of this scenario to the other two, will allow us to identify the combined effect. If it is much wider than both, both sources are important and may reinforce each other. If it is similar to just one scenario, that source is dominant. If it is only slightly larger than the sum of both sources, the sources act independently which suggests that there is no strong interaction.
In summary, by comparing the width of nutrient predictions in each scenario, one can tell whether those nutrients in the system are mainly controlled by point sources, diffuse sources, or a combination of both—and whether their effects interact or not. Min–max envelopes for yearly nutrient loads under the W+S scenario, together with their mean values (black line), are shown in Figure 13. In this case, uncertainty in the S inputs was quantified by constructing envelopes around the mean soil nutrient concentrations using ± the absolute median of the relative interpolation error. In Figure 14 and 15, the min–max bounds are instead derived using the 75th percentile of the relative soil interpolation error, providing a more conservative estimate of uncertainty.

### Width of prediction envelopes

This chapter compares scenario responses using two complementary summaries of the daily prediction envelope. Chart variant 1 reports the relative width (R) of each day t, , where  is the two-member ensemble median, whereas Chart variant 2 reports the corresponding absolute width W, , in physical units such as  for loads and  for concentrations. Both metrics are summarized separately for all days, high-flow days, and baseflow days, using medians of the daily values to provide robust comparisons that are less distorted by extreme-event outliers while still retaining information on proportional sensitivity in the relative-width charts and on practical envelope magnitude in the absolute-width charts.

Because daily SWAT outputs are strongly serially correlated, uncertainty was evaluated using an effective sample size rather than the nominal number of simulated days, adopting the AR(1)-based correction  following Zwiers and von Storch (1995) and Yue and Wang (2004), an approach consistent with common treatment of persistent hydrological series (Li et al., 2016; McInerney et al., 2017; Hernandez-Suarez and Nejadhashemi, 2022; Decremer et al., 2014). For all 14245 sample days effective sample sizes vary depending on the variable and metric compared between neff = 1157-1973 corresponding to lag-1 autocorrelations ( ) of 0.76 to 0.85.
In the bar charts, whiskers represent  for the mean and therefore provide a visual guide to whether apparent differences are likely to be robust; following the “inference by eye” framework, clear non-overlap of 95% confidence intervals suggests strong evidence of separation (Cumming and Finch, 2005; Cumming and Fidler, 2009). The text reports on pairwise contrasts shown in the figures and adds the 95% confidence interval of the difference, , and Hedges’ , which standardizes the difference between two group means by dividing them by their pooled standard deviations (cohens’ d) and applies a small-sample bias correction (Hedges and Olkin, 1985). This metrics combination helps distinguish hydrologically impactful differences from merely numerical ones and provides a more defensible basis for comparing source effects across scenarios, flow regimes, and the inherently different scales of TN and TP (Cohen, 1988; Sawilowsky, 2009; Wasserstein and Lazar, 2016).

### Overall Model–Observation Fit

This section examines the relationship between simulated loads for reach 13 and available measurements of concentrations at the same reach.
Conventional goodness-of-fit indicators applied to the raw measurements were not interpretable due to sparse and heterogeneous sampling. To better describe model–data agreement under these conditions, we use envelope coverage and bias diagnostics, which account for variability in the observations.
Coverage is defined as the fraction of observations lying within the simulated min–max envelope, and mean bias, defined as simulated minus observed concentration. PBIAS is reported only as the relative expression of the same bias, whereas pairwise scenario contrasts in mean bias are summarized by , , and Hedges’ .

> **[FIGURE PLACEHOLDER]** *Figure 23: all days; simulated vs measured concentration coverage and bias stats; TP observations = 25; TN = 18*

Across all days (Fig. 23), fit was strongly nutrient-specific. For TP, the WASTE envelope captured 64.0% of observations, whereas SOIL alone captured only 12.0%; combining both perturbations increased TP coverage only slightly, to 68.0%. For TN, the pattern reversed: WASTE covered 11.1% of observations, whereas SOIL and WASTE+SOIL covered 44.4% and 55.6%, respectively. Thus, the model's ability to bracket TP observations was associated mainly with wastewater perturbations, whereas TN fit depended mainly on soil perturbations. The bias metrics show the same contrast. For TP, all scenarios overestimated concentrations on average, with mean bias increasing fom +0.19 mg L-1 under WASTE to +0.34 mg L-1 under SOIL and +0.70 mg L-1 under WASTE+SOIL; the corresponding PBIAS values were +62.1%, +111%, and +230%. For TN, WASTE underestimated concentrations on average, with a mean bias of -15.3 mg L-1 and a PBIAS of -67.7%, whereas SOIL and WASTE+SOIL both overestimated TN to a similar extent, with mean biases of +12.9 and +12.8 mg L-1 and PBIAS values of +57.2% and +56.5%.
Despite that directional pattern, the all-day pairwise contrasts in mean bias remained imprecise. For TP, the WASTE-SOIL and WASTE-WASTE+SOIL contrasts were Δ = -0.15 and -0.51 mg L-1, with CI95(Δ) of [-0.96, +0.67] and [-1.29, +0.28], and small Hedges' g values of 0.10 and 0.37. For TN, WASTE lay about 28 mg L-1 below both SOIL and WASTE+SOIL, but the corresponding CI95(Δ) intervals [-72.4, +15.9] and [-70.2, +14.1] still included zero. Therefore, the pooled all-day data support a clear directional difference in fit, but not a sharp statistical separation among scenario mean biases.

> **[FIGURE PLACEHOLDER]** *Figure 24: baseflow days; simulated vs measured concentration coverage and bias stats; TP observations = 18; TN = 12*

Under baseflow conditions (Fig. 24), coverage improved and the nutrient-specific pattern became clearer. For TP, WASTE and WASTE+SOIL captured 68.4% and 73.7% of observations, whereas SOIL captured only 5.3%. For TN, coverage increased from 15.4% under WASTE to 61.5% under SOIL and 76.9% under WASTE+SOIL. Baseflow TP bias remained modest: WASTE showed a small positive mean bias (+0.13 mg L-1; +35.9% PBIAS), SOIL a small negative bias (-0.08 mg L-1; -22.5%), and WASTE+SOIL a larger positive bias (+0.35 mg L-1; +99.8%). For TN, however, the contrast was much stronger: WASTE underestimated concentrations (-4.72 mg L-1; -34.1%), whereas SOIL and WASTE+SOIL strongly overestimated them (+33.1 and +32.6 mg L-1; +239% and +235%). Even so, pairwise baseflow contrasts still remained uncertain: for TN, WASTE-SOIL and WASTE-WASTE+SOIL differed by Δ = -37.8 and -37.3 mg L-1, but CI95(Δ) still spanned zero [-88.9, +13.3] and [-84.9, +10.3].

> **[FIGURE PLACEHOLDER]** *Figure 25: high-flow days; simulated vs measured concentration coverage and bias stats; TP observations = 7; TN obersvations = 6*

High-flow conditions (Fig. 25) showed the clearest structural mismatch between model and observations. TP coverage fell to 33.3-50.0%, with WASTE and WASTE+SOIL each bracketing half of the six event observations and SOIL only one third. TN coverage collapsed to 0% under all scenarios. At the same time, TP was overestimated in every scenario, with mean biases of +0.38, +1.65, and +1.78 mg L-1 under WASTE, SOIL, and WASTE+SOIL, corresponding to PBIAS values of +268%, +1150%, and +1250%. TN showed the opposite behavior: all scenarios severely underestimated event concentrations, with mean biases of -42.8, -39.5, and -38.7 mg L-1 and PBIAS values from -94.3% to -85.3%. With only six TP and five TN event observations, pairwise scenario contrasts remained unresolved; even the largest TP contrast, WASTE-WASTE+SOIL, yielded Δ = -1.40 mg L-1, CI95(Δ) [-4.71, +1.91], and Hedges' g = 0.52.
By contrast with those unresolved within-regime scenario contrasts, the between-regime TN bias contrasts were clear. Relative to baseflow, TN mean bias became 38.1 mg L-1 more negative under WASTE, with CI95(Δ) [-50.6, -25.5], and about 72 mg L-1 more negative under SOIL and WASTE+SOIL, with CI95(Δ) [-123, -21.8] and [-119, -24.0], respectively. TP showed no comparably resolved between-regime contrast in mean bias, because the corresponding CI95(Δ) intervals all spanned zero: [-0.98, +0.47] for WASTE, [-5.11, +1.66] for SOIL, and [-4.74, +1.88] for WASTE+SOIL.
Overall, mean-bias behavior was more strongly separated by flow regime than by scenario. Within a given regime, scenario contrasts in mean bias remained statistically unresolved, whereas TN mean bias differed significantly between baseflow and high-flow conditions in all three scenarios. Coverage, however, was numerically affected by perturbation pathway: under baseflow TP coverage ranged from 5.3% to 73.7% and TN coverage from 15.4% to 76.9%, while under high flow TN coverage collapsed to 0% in every scenario. Thus, the main unresolved limitation appears to be the storm response itself rather than the route by which uncertainty is introduced: across all scenarios, TP was overestimated while TN was underestimated during events.

## Flow-sorted concentration/load curves

Flow-sorted concentration and loading curves (load-duration curves) were constructed as follows. For each simulation day, ensemble statistics (minimum, median, and maximum) of the target variable (either TP or TN concentrations or loads evaluated at reach 13) were computed across the available simulation runs and paired with the corresponding daily discharge for that day (which remains fixed across realizations). Days are ranked by ascending discharge and assigned a flow percentile position (0–100 %), such that the x-axis spans conditions from the driest (left) to the wettest (right). The simulated min-max envelopes are plotted against this flow-percentile axis while preserving the day-level pairing between water-quality variables and water discharge; each observed value is placed at the flow percentile corresponding to its calendar date.  The event-zone shading on this chart indicates the approximate flow regime where event conditions typically occur; individual days near the boundary may be misassigned in either direction due to the temporal structure of event detection (consecutive-day rule) not being expressible as a simple flow threshold.. Finally, a 60-day rolling mean is applied to visually smooth the synoptic-scale variability introduced by sorting more than 14,000 simulated loads by flow regime.

> **[FIGURE PLACEHOLDER]** *Figure 26: TN Flow sorted min-max prediction envelope and time-paired WFD measurements for all days. The event-zone shading on this chart indicates the approximate flow regime where event conditions typically occur; individual days near the boundary may be misassigned in either direction due to the temporal structure of event detection (consecutive-day rule) not being expressible as a simple flow threshold. leading to a wider event-day zone; rolling smoothing window: 60 days.*

The W+S TN flow curve in Figure 26 visually confirms the bias sign-reversal quantified earlier (Fig. 23). Across much of the non-event zone, measurements fall within the prediction envelope but below its median, whereas in the event-day zone all observations rise above the max realizations´ envelope. Qualitatively, the figure therefore supports the suggestion that the model represents TN more plausibly under low-flow conditions than during storms, where the highest observed concentrations are not captured by the simulated range.

> **[FIGURE PLACEHOLDER]** *Figure 27: BASE scenario TN concentration predictions sorted by flow percentile, mapped against corresponding measurements; N=18; Bias (obs-pred) = 17,5 ; PBIAS% = 77; Rolling smoothing window: 100 days*

The BASE TN curve in Figure 27 shows a similar pattern which is less clearly partitioned by our event-definition. The separation between observations and the prediction is smaller through much of the low-flow range, but the higher-flow measurements (above P20) considerably sit above the simulated values. This suggests that the high-flow TN mismatch is not introduced by the W+S perturbation itself; rather, the combined scenario mainly preserves and amplifies a general TN overprediction already present in the BASE scenario.

> **[FIGURE PLACEHOLDER]** *Figure 28: TP Flow sorted min-max prediction envelope and time-paired WFD measurements for all days. The event-zone shading on this chart indicates the approximate flow regime where event conditions typically occur; individual days near the boundary may be misassigned in either direction due to the temporal structure of event detection (consecutive-day rule) not being expressible as a simple flow threshold. leading to a wider event-day zone; rolling smoothing window: 60 days.*

The W+S TP curve in Figure 28 shows a different error structure from TN. The simulation envelope remains elevated relative to many observations across the flow spectrum. While the mismatch becomes more pronounced toward the high-flow end, where measured TP concentrations cluster below or near the lower realization, during baseflow conditions most measurements landed within the envelope.

> **[FIGURE PLACEHOLDER]** *Figure 29: BASE scenario TP concentration predictions sorted by flow percentile, mapped against corresponding measurements; N=25; Bias (obs-pred) = 0.19; PBIAS% = 60; Rolling smoothing window: 100 days*

The BASE TP curve in Figure 29 is less uniformly shifted upward than the W+S case. Through baseflow conditions, model output sits below measured TP values, but toward the high-flow end the simulated values do tend to sit above the measurements. Relative to this reference, the W+S perturbation appears to raise the TP prediction band more broadly rather than changing the basic flow-ordering of the mismatch.
Taken together, Figures 26-29 provide a continuous visual complement to the regime-split coverage and bias comparisons (Fig. 23-25). Both suggest that the largest departures between model and measurements occur toward higher flows: for TN, the highest-flow observations tend to exceed the modelled range, whereas for TP the model tends to retain an elevated concentration band above the observed values.
