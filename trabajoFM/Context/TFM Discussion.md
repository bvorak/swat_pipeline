# **1**          **Discussion**

Distinguishing the influence of specific sources under sparse and uncertain measurements benefits from framing results around explicit uncertainty and clearly stated model constraints. The analysis (i) separates **sensitivity signals**—via envelope width and shifts relative to the BASE mean—from **agreement signals** (coverage/bias, treated in §4.3), and (ii) interprets differences between municipal wastewater (point) and soil initialization (diffuse) through their hydrologic pathways. Within this frame, nitrogen (TN) and phosphorus (TP) respond differently across scenarios and flow regimes, providing insight into which inputs most strongly displace the model and when.

Because the measurements are sparse and uncertain, the results are interpreted using simple indicators of model behaviour and agreement. Changes in model response (evaluated in terms of nutrient concentrations) in response to changes  inputs (e.g., envelope width and deviation from the BASE mean) are considered separately from how well the model matches observations (coverage and bias; see §4.3). Differences between wastewater (point source) and soil inputs (diffuse source) are interpreted in terms of their hydrological pathways. Within this framework, TN and TP show different responses across scenarios and flow conditions, helping to identify which inputs have the strongest influence and under which conditions.

This design is consistent with the research objectives stated in §1.3: first, to quantify how sensitive model outputs are to uncertainty in soil composition and wastewater inputs; second, to assess the extent to which those uncertainty ranges explain observed water quality. The choice to prioritize uncertainty-explicit evaluation rather than aggressive tuning follows the guidance that “users should not over-calibrate their models to obtain values of statistical performance measures better than the uncertainty of the available measured data” (Moriasi et al., 2015). Complementing scalar statistics with visualization-led diagnostics also aligns with recommended practice: “Graphical PMs [performance measures] provide an important complementary tool for modelers to support the calibration and validation of H/WQ models (Daggupati et al., 2015a)” (Moriasi et al., 2015b). Here, envelope plots and Load Duration Curves help relate source influences to flow conditions, allowing the objectives to be addressed without exceeding the limits of the available data.

## **1.1**        **Sensitivity analysis**

Sensitivity was evaluated by perturbing inputs in SOIL (initial magnitude of soil N and P pools), WASTE (magnitude of N, P and organic matter point source loadings), and W+S (both) scenarios and comparing their effects on TP and TN in the most downstream reach of Cubillas river upstream the reservoir, relative to BASE simulation. As the BASE model already incorporates defaults for diffuse agricultural loads and typical soil-initialization presets, a perfectly clean comparison of point- versus non-point impacts cannot be made. As Figure 6 shows, the chosen minimal bounds are even lower than what SWAT apparently initializes for soil composition in the BASE scenario. If SWAT did not automatically initialize soils with such presets, the expectation would be that the SOIL scenario’s lower-pollution initialization would result in strictly higher pollution levels than the BASE scenario.

While model and data quality issues reduce the power of statistical comparisons, several consistent insights emerge. For sensitivity analysis the scenarios show clear tendencies. There are clear trends separating continuously introduced point loads from flow-event-dependent diffuse sources. Figures 5 and 6 reveal the relative sensitivities of the model under WASTE versus SOIL. The former affects the model throughout the year to a similar extent, whereas the latter exerts its main influence during load peaks, with negligible variance during low-load periods. This aligns with process expectations: wastewater is introduced to the river continuously with only slightly varying amounts due to population dynamics, whereas soil-bound nutrient and organic loads reach the river primarily via runoff during rain events (source).

Section 4.2.2 shows strongly event-driven TP increases under SOIL (events +401 vs. non-events +17) and largely regime-invariant TN increases under WASTE (events +133; non-events +102). **These patterns reflect source–pathway contrasts: SOIL perturbs TP more than TN, whereas WASTE perturbs TN more than TP.** In SWAT, much of the watershed phosphorus signal is routed with eroded sediment; storm runoff mobilizes particulate and sorbed P from soils and delivers it in short, pulsed exports, so initializing soil P pools naturally amplifies event sensitivity in TP (as observed). This process representation aligns with the model theory and practice documentation indicating P’s close association with runoff and sediment (Chaubey & Migliaccio, 2006; Tetra Tech, 2015). At the catchment scale, empirical patterns likewise show that P export commonly tracks high flows and sediment pulses. In contrast, municipal effluents characteristically carry higher nitrogen than phosphorus on a concentration basis (WASTE assigns TN 20–85 mg L⁻¹ vs. TP 4–15 mg L⁻¹). In addition—and consistent with Section 4.2.1—relative width is generally higher for WASTE and W+S than for SOIL (for example, TP 147% vs. 96%; TN 152% vs. 57%), with the notable exception of TP during events where SOIL exceeds WASTE (76% vs. 67%). Moreover, the combined W+S scenario inherits SOIL-like event sensitivity (TP +427 > TN +377) and WASTE-like non-event sensitivity (TN +107 > TP +41), reinforcing the pathway interpretation set out above.

**Sensitivity: model vs. back of the envelope**

## **1.2**      **Dominant pollution routes: Point vs.** **non-point sources**

            This section evaluates how much of the observed water-quality signal can be explained by the introduced point (WASTE) and diffuse (SOIL) load scenarios and their uncertainty envelopes. The main conclusion is that these scenarios account for a substantial share of the measured pollution, yet notable residual uncertainty remains, as a non-trivial fraction of observations lies outside the envelopes. The interpretation depends on the assumptions towards validation data quality, particularly for **TN**, where either a model bias toward overpredicting low-load conditions (reported below detection limit) or the assumption that reported below-detection-limit (BDL) values are too noisy—can degrade agreement.

            Measurement handling materially affects attribution. Negative measurements were removed. BDL measurements exerted a strong influence on **TN**: including them produced mixed signals because especially uncalibrated model behavior (BASE and DEFAULT) tended to predict very low loads during low-flow periods and could therefore accommodate values close to zero. When BDL entries were excluded, the sediment-calibrated configuration outperformed the uncalibrated alternative clearly, indicating that the quality of the comparison dataset is pivotal for assessing explanatory power.

            Across scenarios, **municipal** **waste inputs explained a meaningful portion of the measured variability****,** and **combining WASTE and SOIL explained even more**, whereas the improvement associated to **perturbations in the soil initialization procedures alone** only improved marginally the model’s capability to predict water quality. When focusing on higher-quality (abode detection limit) data these trends of the model’s explanatory power became more clear, and **W+S** yielded here the best overall performance; however, classical correlation statistics remained mixed and mostly unsatisfactory likely due to the small sample size. In other words, modest bias reduction and increased envelope coverage represent a pragmatic, if conservative, signal of improvement under the data limitations. The quantitative patterns are consistent with these conclusions.  
            For **TP**, coverage and bias [[U2]](#_msocom_2) improve monotonically from **SOIL** **→** **WASTE** **→** **W+S****;** **W+S** attains the highest TP coverage (**77.8%** in the all-data subset; **87.5%** in the above-DL subset) with low bias (e.g., **1.7; 5.5%** in all-data**;** **4.41; 12.4%** in above-DL).  
For **TN**, performance is highly sensitive to BDL handling (11 entries)[[U3]](#_msocom_3) . Under **W+S****,** removing BDL values flips the bias sign (from **–51.8%** to **+40.4%****)** and increases coverage from **30%** to **66.7%****.** These results indicate that **taking WASTE and SOIL uncertainty together provides the best overall envelope** **coverage**, while underscoring that the confidence in TN attribution hinges on rigorous treatment of low-level measurements.

            In summary, the introduced **point** and **diffuse** load scenarios—used with explicit uncertainty envelopes—substantially improve the model’s ability to bracket observations, especially for **TP** under **W+S** and for **TN** once BDL values are handled consistently. Nevertheless, the persistence of observations outside the envelopes confirms that additional uncertainty sources and process representations must be addressed before a well-fitted model can be claimed.

## **1.3**      **Reflections on the method**

            The principal challenge is the **mismatch** **between** **validation** **data** **resolution** **and** **model** **resolution**. Water-quality measurements are sparse, unevenly distributed in time, and biased, predominantly representing baseflow conditions (slowly varying low-flow rates). The model, in turn, simulates day-to-day variations and, in particular, represents how the system reacts to short-term events, such as storms or high-flow periods, which can cause rapid changes in water quality. SWAT inputs though are equally sparse and derived through heuristic interpolation. This discrepancy complicates attribution and can make envelope coverage appear poorer than the underlying process representation. In addition, **uncontrolled** **discharges**—for example, irrigation returns, overflows, and small diversions—are not explicitly represented; these pathways likely alter local concentrations and should be considered when interpreting model–observation differences.

            Testing the load model benefits from strict separation of training and test data to avoid overfitting, as well as from independent lines of evidence. Satellite water-quality proxies (e.g., turbidity, chlorophyll-a) offer synoptic context for load regimes and event timing, while drone-based measurements could provide flexible, targeted sampling to reduce spatial gaps. For hindcast GHG emissions, two complementary validation avenues are apparent: (i) sediment cores for historical constraints—including microbial community signals (e.g., methanogenic archaea)—and (ii) real-time flux observations from the platform at Embalse de Cubillas (methane, water vapour, CO₂, light, and submerged sensors), with explicit attention to the time–space resolution of both datasets[[U4]](#_msocom_4) .

            Interpolation choices used for initializing soil pools performed as intended for spatial averaging but have known side effects at extremes. Although cross-validation diagnostics indicated unbiased and well-calibrated interpolation (RMSSE ≈ 1.0; RMSE ≈ ASE),[[U5]](#_msocom_5)  scatterplots revealed a systematic underestimation of high concentrations due to the skewed distribution of soil chemistry, where a few hotspots dominate the range[[U6]](#_msocom_6) . Kriging thus provided reliable HRU-scale means but smoothed hotspots, and this limitation was explicitly acknowledged when propagating uncertainty to the SWAT ensembles as described in the methodology chapter[[U7]](#_msocom_7) .

### 1.3.1         Strengths of this approach

In this approach, SWAT  is used to predict a range (envelopes) of possible concentration values in the river and loadings into the Cubillas resevoir instead of a single number. Different sources of uncertainty are introduced, including the magnitude of the intital nutrient pools (SOIL), and point-source loadings (WASTE). The model is run multiple times, each time changing only the source of nutrients (either SOIL, WASTE, or both together W+S), while keeping everything else the same. This makes it easy to see how strongly the model responds to each source (spread) and how much results change compared to the baseline simulations.  Load Duration Curves are used to compare model performance under different flow conditions, such as low-flow (baseflow) and high-flow (event) periods. Even with few and irregular measurements, LDCs help show overall patterns and allow the model to be evaluated for both baseflow and event conditions.

### 1.3.2        Limitations of this approach and outlook

            We identify several limitations that qualify our findings, yet they also suggest clear next steps. First, the limited number of measurements and the fact that most samples were taken during low-flow (baseflow) periods reduce the statistical strength of the analysis. The approach followed to handle below-detection-limit (BDL) values has a major impact on TN results, with goodness-of-fit metrics indicating overprediction or underprediction depending on which BDL method is selected. To help address these limitations in the future, a high-frequency monitoring campaign would be required upstream of the Cubillas reservoir to complement official DMA records and better capture variability during critical flow conditions. Second, Accurate representation of peak flows and sediment-associated phosphorus likely needs focused calibration to improve TP predictions during events. In addition, related in-stream retention parameters should be carefully considered. Third, the current **min–max bands are conservative**; errors need not add linearly, so a **Monte Carlo** propagation is a fitting refinement. Fourth, **SWAT was configured to simulate the natural regime of river flows, and does not account for water diversions in (Colomera-Cubillas irrigation channel dicharging hypolimnetic water from Colomera reservoir into Cubillas river upstream the sampling sites in summer) and out (Deifontes spring being used for drinking water supply outside the catchment) of the Cubillas system. These diversions could affect significantly the magnitude of the concentrations and, therefore, might explain the differences between observed and simulated TN and TP.** Fifth, the current **simulation period** does not cover the more recent, higher-quality monitoring (post-2020), limiting overlap.[[U8]](#_msocom_8) 

            Hydraulic structures and diversions near gauges constrain comparability because **water-quality modelling is systematically dependent on accurate flow and sediment regimes**. Given scarce data and the limited high-resolution insights obtained here, it is arguable that, for some screening tasks, a **simpler watershed model (e.g., GWLF)** could suffice, provided uncertainties are treated explicitly.

**Priorities** include expanding event-condition sampling; reporting results under **dual filters** (all-data and above-DL) for nitrogen metrics; and targeting **sediment/erosion** and **in-stream retention** parameters. The same uncertainty framework can be applied to **agricultural inputs and management practices** to compare their explanatory power with **soil and wastewater** uncertainties, and to disentangle **anthropogenic pollution** from **soil/native vegetation** signals. Integration of satellite and drone observations, together with the GHG platform data (Collados-Lara et al., 2023), offers a coherent pathway to strengthen validation across spatial and temporal scales.[[U9]](#_msocom_9)
